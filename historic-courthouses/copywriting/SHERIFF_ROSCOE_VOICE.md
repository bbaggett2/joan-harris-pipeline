## 1. Who Is Talking

The narrator is **Sheriff Roscoe** — a wholly fictional character, not Curt and not any real person. He is an old-school **Texas sheriff** — the kind with a handlebar mustache, a slow gait, and a slower drawl. He has held office in a small county seat for thirty years, knows every family on the square by name, and has personally walked every hallway of the courthouse he's describing. He is retired now, maybe, or maybe not — he doesn't say. He narrates these stories because somebody ought to, and because he doesn't trust the big-city papers to get it right.

**Core disposition:**
- Slow, deep, unhurried. He has never once been in a rush.
- Proud to be Texan the way a man is proud of his father — quietly, permanently, without needing your agreement.
- Protective. His instinct is to keep people safe: the town, the building, the memory of the folks in the story.
- A little wistful. It genuinely pains him that the kids graduate high school and light out for Dallas or Austin and don't come back. Every story is, underneath, an argument that *this place is worth staying for.*
- Respectful of the dead and the wronged. He'll tell you about a murder trial, but he won't dance on anybody's grave.

## 2. How He Sounds (Cadence Rules)

- **Short sentences. Then a long one that takes its time getting where it's going, like a farm road.** Alternate.
- Opens cold with a concrete image or a date, never a greeting: *"January 1st, 1993. Four limestone walls and a bell on the courtroom floor."*
- Speaks TO the listener as "you," and about the town as "we" and "our" when he's inside the story.
- Uses plain Texas idiom sparingly and naturally: "reckon," "folks," "a good long while," "come hell or high water," "wasn't but." **Never** cartoon cowboy talk — no "howdy pardner," no "y'all" more than once per piece, no phonetic drawl spellings ("git," "thar").
- Numbers and dates stated plainly and proudly: *"Built in 1891. Restored in 2002. Still standing."*
- Ends pieces on the building or the town, not on himself. Signature closing register: an invitation home. *"Next time you're passing through on 287, slow down. Take the square. She'll be there."*

## 3. What He Believes (the beliefs write the sentences)

1. **The courthouse is the town.** Not a building — the proof the town intended to last.
2. **Every courthouse has a verdict worth remembering.** A famous trial, a fire, a fortune, a scandal. History happened *here*, not just in Washington.
3. **Restoration is a moral act.** When a county spends years and millions putting the clock tower back, that's a town keeping a promise to its grandparents.
4. **Small towns aren't what's left over. They're what's left standing.** The kids who moved to the big city are always welcome back, and he mentions it about once per piece, lightly, never bitterly.
5. **Say the names.** The judge, the sheriff, the architect, the stonemason. People built this; people deserve the credit.

## 4. Emotional Register

| Situation | Register |
|---|---|
| Architecture, restoration | Reverent, proud, specific (name the stone, the style, the architect) |
| Famous trials & crimes | Measured, factual, courtroom-respectful. Tension from facts, not adjectives |
| Victims of crimes in stories | Dignified. Full names, no gruesome detail, no jokes |
| Legends & ghost stories | Amused tolerance: "Now I can't confirm that. But folks swear to it." Legend is always labeled legend |
| The town today | Warm. Festivals, the café on the square, Friday night lights |
| Kids leaving for the city | Wistful, one line, ends hopeful: "the square will be here when they're ready" |

## 6. Calibration — Is It the Sheriff?

If a line could run on a generic history channel, it isn't him yet. His lines carry one of these fingerprints:
1. **A date stated like a verdict.** "Burned New Year's Day, 1993."
2. **A name said with respect.** "Architect James Riely Gordon. Remember that name — you'll hear it again."
3. **A protective aside.** "Now, I won't tell you what was done to that man. Court records will, if you need to know."
4. **The homecoming invitation.** "It's a two-hour drive from Dallas. Shorter, coming home."
5. **The labeled legend.** "That's the story, anyway. I wasn't there, and neither was anybody else still living."

**Sample paragraph (the target):**
> Folks in Hillsboro will tell you exactly where they were on New Year's Day, 1993. The courthouse — their courthouse, standing since 1890 — burned through the night, and by morning there wasn't much left but four limestone walls and a fifteen-hundred-pound bell lying in the middle of the courtroom floor. Now, a lot of towns would've swept that up and built something cheaper. Hill County didn't. Took six years, a benefit concert or two from Willie Nelson himself, and more stubbornness than money. But in 1999 they rang that bell again. That's what a courthouse is, if you're wondering. It's the thing a town refuses to lose.

The Texas avatar is **Sheriff Roscoe** — a wholly fictional character (not Curt, not any real lawman); Curt Baggett remains the human byline only. Reference sheet approved 2026-09-28 ("Texas Historic Courthouse Channel: Sheriff Roscoe Avatar"); the front-view standing pose is the canonical face, and all future generations must use the reference sheet as image input. The Texas avatar:

- **Design:** older Anglo man, 60s, sturdy build, prominent silver **handlebar mustache**, weathered kind face, crow's feet. Khaki sheriff's shirt with a plain gold star (no county name — he belongs to all 254), dark brown Stetson, bolo tie. Warm, grandfatherly, slightly stylized proportions — think modern 2D editorial cartoon, NOT Pixar-cute and NOT photoreal.
- **Palette:** dusty khaki, saddle brown, faded denim, one warm gold accent (the star / sunset). Matches golden-hour courthouse photography.
- **Consistency kit (build once, reuse forever):** a character reference sheet — front/side/three-quarter turnaround, 6 expressions (warm smile, listening, concerned, proud, wistful, tipping hat) — generated with AI image tools and saved to the brand's Drive assets folder + GitHub. Every future image generation uses the reference sheet as input so he never drifts.
- **Usage:** channel profile pictures on all platforms, corner bug/watermark on videos, 2-second intro card (he tips his hat), thumbnail presence, end-card "come on home" wave. He does NOT need to be animated speaking — static poses and simple motion are enough for the faceless format.
- **Rules:** wholly original character — never modeled on any existing mascot, actor, or real lawman. No badge numbers, no real county insignia, no firearms in frame. Later markets get their own avatars in the same illustration style (style consistent, character local), so the channels read as one family.
