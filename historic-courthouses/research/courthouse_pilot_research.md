# Texas Historic Courthouse Pilot Research — 12 Courthouses

This document profiles 12 restored, photogenic Texas county courthouses selected for story strength and geographic spread, for use in daily blog articles and 60–90 second faceless documentary videos. All facts below were verified against the listed sources; unconfirmed claims are flagged under each courthouse's TO VERIFY line.

## Ellis County Courthouse — Waxahachie, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Ellis County, Waxahachie — North Texas (about 30 miles south of Dallas).
2. **Built:** 1897. Architect: James Riely Gordon (on-site supervision by Messer, Sanguinet and Messer). Style: Romanesque Revival. Materials include gray granite, rose pink granite, Burnet County limestone, Pecos red sandstone, and a slate roof. Original cost approximately $130,000 plus architect's fees (per Ellis County's official history page).
3. **Restoration:** Full restoration 2000–2002 under the Texas Historic Courthouse Preservation Program; the county reports a $3.57 million THC matching grant (2000) and a total restoration cost of $10–12 million. Rededicated October 2002 (THC).
4. **THE STORY — The Faces of Mabel Frame:** In 1894–95, stone carvers were brought in to sculpt the ornamental faces on the courthouse's porches — 21 carved stone faces in all. Local legend says carver Harry Herley fell in love with Mabel (Mable) Frame, daughter of the owner of the boarding house where he stayed, and carved her beautiful likeness over an entrance; when she rejected him, he carved her again and again as a snarling, twisted demon around the building. Historians are skeptical: the faces match traditional European Romanesque grotesques, the carvings were probably made in Dallas and shipped in, and records show Herley married Minnie Hodges in 1896 and likely died by 1899. True or not, the legend is the single most retold courthouse story in Texas — even the county's own website recounts it. Author James A. Michener called the building "a fairy tale palace... replete with battlements and turrets and spires."
5. **Notable facts:** One of Gordon's best-known buildings (THC). Waxahachie is nicknamed "The Best Little Hollywood in Texas" — Oscar-winning films Bonnie and Clyde (1967), Tender Mercies (1983), Places in the Heart (1984), and The Trip to Bountiful (1985) were shot in and around town; more than 30 films/TV shows total (KERA). Also called the "Gingerbread City" for its Victorian architecture.
6. **Local pride hooks:** "Best Little Hollywood in Texas," "Gingerbread City," "Crape Myrtle Capital of Texas"; the carved-faces legend is told to every visitor on the square.
7. **Sources:**

   - https://www.elliscountytx.gov/460/Our-Historic-Court-House
   - https://www.roadsideamerica.com/story/10855
   - https://www.kera.org/artandseek/three-oscar-winning-films-helped-make-waxahachie-the-best-little-hollywood-in-texas/

## Hood County Courthouse — Granbury, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Hood County, Granbury — North Texas (southwest of Fort Worth).
2. **Built:** 1890–91. Architect: W.C. Dodson, one of Texas' most prolific courthouse architects. Style: French Second Empire, limestone with ornamental sheet-metal tower and cornice; original 1890 Seth Thomas clockworks still hand-wound monthly.
3. **Restoration:** THCPP planning grant $344,171 (Round II, 2001); full restoration construction grant of $5,000,000 (Round V, 2008), with $4,793,916 contributed to the project. Work ran 2008–2011; rededicated October 27, 2012. Restorers uncovered hand-painted designs on five vault doors and two safes and restored ornamental plaster paintings in the District Courtroom (THC).
4. **THE STORY — The Bartender Who Said He Was John Wilkes Booth:** In the 1870s a theatrical, Shakespeare-quoting liquor and tobacco merchant named John St. Helen worked on the Granbury square — lawyer Finis L. Bates met him in Granbury in 1873. In 1878, believing he was dying, St. Helen confessed to Bates: "My name is John Wilkes Booth, and I am the assassin of President Lincoln." He recovered, elaborated the story, and vanished. In 1903 a man called David E. George died of strychnine poisoning in Enid, Oklahoma (January 13, 1903), leaving papers asking that Bates be summoned; Bates identified the body as St. Helen, later toured the mummified corpse in sideshows as "Booth's mummy," and published The Escape and Suicide of John Wilkes Booth in 1907. Finis Bates was the grandfather of actress Kathy Bates. The building where St. Helen allegedly tended bar still stands on the square, and a grave marked "Jesse James" sits just off the square — Granbury markets both mysteries to this day.
5. **Notable facts:** Centerpiece of the Hood County Courthouse Historic District (NRHP). A 1968 tornado nearly cost the courthouse its clock tower — a grassroots campaign led by the Hood County News publisher convinced commissioners to repair rather than remove it, sparking Granbury's preservation movement (THC).
6. **Local pride hooks:** Granbury promotes itself as a "Best Historic Small Town" destination built around the square; 28 historic structures downtown; the Booth and Jesse James legends are core local lore featured on TV series.
7. **Sources:**
   - https://thc.texas.gov/preserve/preservation-programs/courthouse-preservation/restored-historic-courthouses/hood-county
   - https://www.visitgranbury.com/blog/post/history-and-legends-of-granbury/
   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finis_L._Bates
   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hood_County_Courthouse_Historic_District

TO VERIFY: That St. Helen specifically worked as a bartender in a still-standing square building (visitgranbury.com asserts it; primary documentation is thin); the exact NRHP listing date for the historic district; USA Today "Best Historic Small Town" award year.

## Fayette County Courthouse — La Grange, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Fayette County, La Grange — Central Texas, roughly midway between Austin and Houston.
2. **Built:** 1891. Architect: James Riely Gordon. Style: Romanesque Revival. Builder: Martin, Byrne & Johnston. Originally featured an open central atrium with a fountain, cast-iron sculptures, and tropical plants for natural light and ventilation.
3. **Restoration:** THCPP restoration June 2003 – March 2005; the enclosed atrium was reopened and restored to its historic condition — THC calls it "one of the most uniquely beautiful features to be found in a courthouse in this state."
4. **THE STORY — The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas:** La Grange was home to the Chicken Ranch, a brothel that operated with local tolerance from 1844 until August 1, 1973 — nearly 130 years. Sheriff T.J. Flournoy, elected 1946, kept a direct phone line to the ranch, fingerprinted new employees, and used intelligence gathered there to solve crimes across Fayette County for 34 years. In mid-1973, Houston TV reporter Marvin Zindler ran a week-long exposé alleging organized-crime ties; a two-month DPS surveillance found no such connections, but the publicity forced Governor Dolph Briscoe to order the ranch closed. Flournoy drove to Austin with a petition signed by nearly 3,000 locals — and found his meeting with the governor canceled. The saga became the Broadway musical and film The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas, with final madam Edna Milton appearing in the stage production. The courthouse, sheriff's office, and county government at its center make the Gordon landmark the natural anchor for the story.
5. **Notable facts:** NRHP listed January 23, 1975; Texas State Antiquities Landmark (1981); Recorded Texas Historic Landmark (2001); anchor of the Fayette County Courthouse Square Historic District (NRHP, 2001).
6. **Local pride hooks:** The restored atrium courthouse; the square historic district; La Grange's place in Texas-Czech heritage country; the town's wry ownership of the Chicken Ranch legend (ZZ Top's "La Grange" is about it).
7. **Sources:**
   - https://thc.texas.gov/preserve/preservation-programs/courthouse-preservation/restored-historic-courthouses/fayette-county
   - https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/chicken-ranch
   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fayette_County_Courthouse_and_Jail

## Hill County Courthouse — Hillsboro, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Hill County, Hillsboro — North Central Texas (Blackland Prairie, I-35 between Waco and Fort Worth).
2. **Built:** 1890. Architect: W.C. Dodson. Style: Second Empire — limestone, Corinthian columns, mansard roofs, and a seven-story clock tower. NRHP listed June 21, 1971; Recorded Texas Historic Landmark 1964.
3. **Restoration:** Reconstructed 1993–1999 after the fire (see story); rededicated April 24, 1999, with then-Governor George W. Bush giving the keynote. Architexas prepared restoration documents from fire fragments and residents' historic photographs; ISTEA federal funds helped pay for it. The project became a catalyst for the creation of the THCPP itself (established 1999).
4. **THE STORY — The Fire and Willie Nelson's Rescue:** In the early hours of January 1, 1993, an electrical fire gutted the beloved 1890 courthouse — only the four limestone walls were left standing, and the collapsing 70-foot bell tower dropped its 1,525-pound Baltimore-cast bell into the district courtroom below. Hill County native Willie Nelson (born in nearby Abbott) announced benefit concerts within weeks — UPI reported the first rebuilding concert set for March 1993 — and donations came in from around the world. Six years and multiple construction phases later, the courthouse was rededicated on April 24, 1999. The disaster is widely credited with galvanizing statewide courthouse preservation: THC's Texas Historic Courthouse Preservation Program launched the same year.
5. **Notable facts:** Won a "Best Restoration" award in 1999 (Downtown Association, per Wikipedia); Hillsboro is cited as a model for historic downtown revitalization (Architexas); support from Laura Bush noted by Architexas.
6. **Local pride hooks:** The Willie Nelson connection (Abbott is in Hill County); the phoenix-from-the-ashes rebuild story; antique-shopping courthouse square.
7. **Sources:**
   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_County_Courthouse
   - https://architexas.com/project-spotlight-hill-county-courthouse/
   - https://www.upi.com/Archives/1993/02/28/Willie-Nelson-courthouse-rebuilding-concert-set-for-March/5834730875600/

## Gonzales County Courthouse — Gonzales, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Gonzales County, Gonzales — South Central Texas ("Come and Take It" city, birthplace of the Texas Revolution).
2. **Built:** Contract let June 1894; completed April 8, 1898, at a cost of $64,450 (Wikipedia; Volz & Associates dates the building 1896). Architect: J. Riely Gordon. Style: Romanesque Revival in red St. Louis brick with white limestone trim.
3. **Restoration:** Two-phase historic restoration completed in the late 1990s (1997 per Wikipedia), partially funded by a federal ISTEA grant — predating the THCPP by several years. It won the THC's Award of Excellence in Historic Architecture in 1999. Restorers repaired lightning and water damage to the central clock tower. NRHP listed June 19, 1972.
4. **THE STORY — The Cursed Clock:** In 1921, Albert Howard was sentenced to hang in Gonzales — reportedly the county's last legal hanging. Maintaining his innocence to the end, he swore that after his death the four faces of the courthouse clock would never again agree, as proof of his wrongful conviction. Local lore holds that the clock faces stopped keeping the same time after the execution — and that lightning struck the tower twice, damaging the mechanism after each repair. The "cursed clock" remains one of the most repeated courthouse legends in Texas and a staple of regional ghost-story roundups, alongside the reputedly haunted 1887 Old Jail Museum across the street.
5. **Notable facts:** Recorded Texas Historic Landmark (1966); Texas State Antiquities Landmark (1981); the courthouse anchors one of Texas' most historic squares — Gonzales is where the first shot of the Texas Revolution was fired (October 1835).
6. **Local pride hooks:** "Come and Take It" celebration each October; the cursed clock story; the Old Jail Museum ghost tours.
7. **Sources:**
   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzales_County_Courthouse
   - https://www.chron.com/news/article/Gonzales-Texas-early-days-and-old-Gonzales-2054986.php
   - https://volzassociates.com/projects/gonzales-county-courthouse/
   - https://www.gvec.org/haunting-tales-and-spooky-places-in-gvec-communities/

## Donley County Courthouse — Clarendon, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Donley County, Clarendon — Texas Panhandle, southeast of Amarillo.
2. **Built:** 1891, by Troutman Brothers for $28,500. Architects: C.H. Bulger and Isaac Hamilton Rapp of Trinidad, Colorado. Style: Romanesque Revival with an unusual asymmetrical design — contrasting red brick and quarried limestone with prominent towers and chimneys.
3. **Restoration:** THCPP Round I construction grant of $3.1 million; restoration completed July 2003. Crews reconstructed the third floor and missing towers and dormers from historic photographs (a 1930s tornado and water damage had led the county to remove the third floor and tower in 1937), reinstalled the stained glass, and relocated a bat colony that had left 18-inch mounds of guano in the attic. THC board member Shirley Caldwell dubbed the result the "Jewel of the Plains."
4. **THE STORY — The Last Hanging in the Panhandle, in the Sobriety City:** Clarendon was founded in 1878 as a Methodist "sobriety settlement" — cowboys mockingly called it "Saints' Roost." Its 1891 courthouse, the oldest still-operating courthouse in the Panhandle, hosted the November 1909 murder trial of G.R. Miller, which ended in the last legal hanging in the Texas Panhandle. Nearly a century later, the building itself became a courtroom drama: decades of leaks, a lost third floor, and a bat infestation had reduced the Panhandle's grandest building to a shell, until a $3.1 million THCPP restoration brought back its towers in 2003. Locals compared watching the scaffolding come down to "watching a beautiful Christmas present being unwrapped." Its vaults still hold JA Ranch records from the 1870s — the ranch co-founded by legendary cattleman Charles Goodnight.
5. **Notable facts:** Oldest functioning courthouse in the Texas Panhandle (THC); over 130 years of county records stored on site, including JA Ranch documents; one of the first THCPP Round I restorations.
6. **Local pride hooks:** "Saints' Roost" founding story and the Saints' Roost Museum; Clarendon's claim to the Panhandle's oldest courthouse; annual Saints' Roost Celebration (July 4).
7. **Sources:**
   - https://thc.texas.gov/preserve/preservation-programs/courthouse-preservation/restored-historic-courthouses/donley-county
   - https://texastimetravel.com/directory/donley-county-courthouse/
   - https://www.ntxe-news.com/cgi-bin/artman/exec/view.cgi?archive=72&num=110046

Miller case details beyond the Texas Time Travel summary; whether Donley's was the first completed THCPP full restoration.

## Shelby County Courthouse — Center, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Shelby County, Center — Deep East Texas, near the Louisiana line.
2. **Built:** 1885, for $26,000. Architect: John Joseph Emmett Gibson, an Irish immigrant. Style: Romanesque Revival modeled on the castles of his native Ireland — turrets, towers, and a frame cupola. It is the only remaining Irish castle-style courthouse in the United States. NRHP listed 1971.
3. **Restoration:** The building is preserved and open for public tours, housing the Shelby County Veterans Office; county judicial business moved to a modern courthouse. County-level restoration reviews have been reported by the Center Light and Champion.
4. **THE STORY — A Castle Born of Fire and Feud:** Shelby County was the battleground of the Regulator–Moderator War (1840–1844), an armed feud over fraudulent land titles that killed dozens and wrecked the local economy — land that had sold for over $2 an acre fell to ten cents. In June 1882, the county's wooden courthouse and all its records burned. The replacement, finished in 1885, was unlike anything else in America: Irish immigrant J.J.E. Gibson built the county a small Romanesque castle, complete with turrets, for $26,000. Today it stands as the only Irish castle-style courthouse in the United States — a fortress of a building for a county that had needed one.
5. **Notable facts:** Only Irish-castle courthouse in the U.S. (Texas Time Travel); National Register property; open for tours.
6. **Local pride hooks:** "The Irish castle courthouse"; Center's poultry-industry identity and East Texas Poultry Festival on the square each fall (local claim); courthouse square anchors downtown.
7. **Sources:**
   - https://texastimetravel.com/directory/shelby-county-courthouse/
   - https://countyprogress.com/monuments-of-justice-shelby-county-courthouse/
   - https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/shelby-county

TO VERIFY: THCPP grant involvement and any formal restoration dates/costs (not confirmed — county has reviewed restoration options per Center Light and Champion); the East Texas Poultry Festival details; popular legends that Gibson was paid partly in cattle or vanished after construction (not found in reliable sources); Sam Houston's role in ending the Regulator-Moderator War (commonly told, not confirmed in TSHA excerpt used).

## Harrison County Courthouse — Marshall, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Harrison County, Marshall — East Texas.
2. **Built:** 1900–1901. Architect: J. Riely Gordon — his first Neo-Classical/Beaux-Arts courthouse, marking his turn away from Romanesque. Corn-yellow brick and carved Lueders limestone with pink granite columns, terra cotta capitals, a stained-glass interior cupola, bronze eagles along the roofline, and a Lady Justice statue atop the dome.
3. **Restoration:** THCPP restoration reopened enclosed porches, restored the double-height courtroom, and recreated decorative finishes; rededicated June 20, 2009. Served as county courthouse until 1964; now houses the Harrison County Historical Museum (est. 2010 in the building) and county offices.
4. **THE STORY — The Eagles That Got Their Wings Back:** The old Harrison County Courthouse is crowned by a Goddess of Justice and ringed by eagles — and in 1965, workers deliberately bent the eagles' wings downward to cut wind friction. For four decades the birds sat clipped, until the full restoration: in 2004, local students ran an "Eagles for Eagles" fundraiser to help pay for replica eagles, and in 2009 the wings went upright again as the building was rededicated. Each winter the courthouse becomes the glowing centerpiece of Marshall's Wonderland of Lights, one of Texas' best-known holiday festivals, when thousands of lights outline the dome — the image most used to represent East Texas in travel literature (Library of Congress).
5. **Notable facts:** The Library of Congress calls it "the signature landmark of Marshall... frequently used to represent East Texas in travel literature"; KLTV calls it "an icon of Texas."
6. **Local pride hooks:** Wonderland of Lights festival each winter; the museum inside; Marshall's claim as a historic East Texas capital of politics and rail.
7. **Sources:**

   - https://theclio.com/entry/62476

   - https://www.kltv.com/2021/07/01/historic-harrison-county-courthouse-is-considered-an-icon-texas/

## Caldwell County Courthouse — Lockhart, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Caldwell County, Lockhart — Central Texas, 30 miles south of Austin.
2. **Built:** 1893–94 (cornerstone August 15, 1893; completed March 19, 1894). Design: Henry E.M. Guidon (long attributed to Alfred Giles; Guidon later became Giles' partner); built by Martin, Byrne and Johnston. Style: Second Empire — cream limestone and red sandstone, mansard roofs, corner towers, and a central clock tower with a four-faced Seth Thomas clock and 900-pound bell. Nearly identical twin of the Goliad County Courthouse.
3. **Restoration:** Restored in 2000 with THCPP matching-grant support (Ford, Powell & Carson): wood windows, masonry, and mansard roofs repaired; railings and ornamental metalwork recreated from historic photographs; original polychrome paint scheme recreated from paint analysis. NRHP: contributing property of the Caldwell County Courthouse Historic District (listed January 3, 1978).
4. **THE STORY — Hollywood's Favorite Texas Courthouse, in the Barbecue Capital:** Lockhart's courthouse may be the most filmed in Texas: its exterior appears in What's Eating Gilbert Grape (1993) and Waiting for Guffman (1996), and the square was a primary filming location for HBO's The Leftovers (seasons 2–3). In 1999 the Texas Legislature officially proclaimed Lockhart the "Barbecue Capital of Texas," home to legendary pits like Kreuz Market, Black's, Smitty's, and Chisholm Trail. And the town's origin story is pure Texas: the Battle of Plum Creek (1840), fought nearby, was a decisive Texan victory over the Comanche. Courthouse, brisket, and film crews share one square.
5. **Notable facts:** Featured in multiple films/TV (Wikipedia); "Barbecue Capital of Texas" by 1999 legislative proclamation; historic district on the NRHP.
6. **Local pride hooks:** Four historic barbecue institutions within blocks of the courthouse; Chisholm Trail Roundup festival (local claim); film-location bragging rights.
7. **Sources:**

## Parker County Courthouse — Weatherford, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Parker County, Weatherford — North Texas, west of Fort Worth.
2. **Built:** 1886. Architect: W.C. Dodson (companion in style to his Hill, Hood, and Lampasas courthouses). Second Empire (per Dodson's body of work); its district courtroom is one of the largest in the state.
3. **Restoration:** Exterior restored in the early 1990s; THCPP-era interior restoration restored the district courtroom to its original size, recreated wall and ceiling paintings and wood balconies, and replaced all building systems; rededicated June 4, 2005 (THC).
4. **THE STORY — The Real Lonesome Dove Homecoming:** Weatherford's Greenwood Cemetery, a short ride from the courthouse square, holds Oliver Loving — the cattle-trail pioneer who blazed the Goodnight–Loving Trail with Charles Goodnight in 1866. On a third drive in spring 1867, Loving rode ahead of the herd, was wounded in a Comanche attack, and died of gangrene at Fort Sumner, New Mexico, on September 25, 1867. Honoring his dying wish to be buried in Texas, Goodnight had his partner's body carried some 600 miles home; Loving was reinterred at Weatherford on March 4, 1868, with Masonic honors. The episode — a cowboy carrying his best friend's body home across the plains — is the story every Texan knows from Lonesome Dove. The courthouse that rose two decades later still presides over Loving's town.
5. **Notable facts:** Rededicated 2005 under the courthouse preservation effort; the square hosts the Parker County Peach Festival (since 1985, second Saturday of July, in the open area surrounding the courthouse).
6. **Local pride hooks:** Peach Festival on the square; Weatherford's cattle-trail heritage; hometown claims to Broadway star Mary Martin (local claim).
7. **Sources:**

   - https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/loving-oliver
   - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_County_Peach_Festival

## Presidio County Courthouse — Marfa, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Presidio County, Marfa — Far West Texas / Big Bend region.
2. **Built:** 1886, for about $60,000–$65,000 (sources differ slightly). Architect: Alfred Giles of San Antonio, who beat eighteen competitors for the commission. Style: Second Empire with Italianate elements — locally made brick and stone, pink stucco exterior, pecan wood interiors, and a Lady Justice statue atop the central dome. The county celebrated completion with a grand ball on New Year's Eve 1886 / January 1, 1887.
3. **Restoration:** Exterior plastered in 1915/1925 alterations; comprehensive renovation completed in spring 2000 (2001 per Wikipedia) including roof, dome, and statue restoration, ADA modifications, and systems updates. NRHP listed December 20, 1977; Recorded Texas Historic Landmark (1964); Texas State Antiquities Landmark (1981).
4. **THE STORY — The Pink Courthouse of Giant Country:** In 1885 the county seat moved from Fort Davis to the railroad town of Marfa, and the county answered with a statement: a $60,000 Second Empire palace in pink, visible from almost anywhere in town, opened with a grand ball on New Year's Eve 1886. Seven decades later, Hollywood came to its doorstep — Giant (1956), with James Dean, Elizabeth Taylor, and Rock Hudson, was filmed around Marfa in 1955, putting the town on the map it never left. Add the Marfa Lights, the mystery glow first recorded by rancher Robert Ellison in 1883, and the courthouse now presides over one of the most improbable art-and-legend towns in America — county population about 6,100, cultural footprint global.
5. **Notable facts:** Called one of the state's best-preserved courthouses (Texas County Progress); Marfa is a world-famous minimalist-art destination (Chinati Foundation) and film location; the dome view and Lady Justice are signature photo subjects.
6. **Local pride hooks:** Marfa Lights Festival (local claim); Giant film heritage and the Hotel Paisano; "visible from anywhere in Marfa" courthouse pride.

## Red River County Courthouse — Clarksville, TX

1. **County/Town/Region:** Red River County, Clarksville — Northeast Texas.
2. **Built:** 1885. Architect: W.H. Wilson. Style: Renaissance Revival with Second Empire elements — limestone, mansard-roofed clock tower, corner columns. It is the only intact surviving work of architect W.H. Wilson.
3. **Restoration:** Full THCPP-era restoration addressing water damage, interior finishes, and life-safety systems; rededicated October 26, 2002 (THC), with county celebration reported in October 2003 (Texas County Progress). Restoration architects: Architexas.
4. **THE STORY — The Sole Survivor:** Every other building W.H. Wilson designed has been lost or altered beyond recognition — the 1885 Red River County Courthouse in Clarksville is the only intact work of his career. By the 1990s the courtroom needed emergency shoring and the stone was crumbling; the THCPP restoration brought the limestone landmark back, and the county rededicated it in 2002. Unusually, it stands in a residential neighborhood several blocks from the commercial square, a quirk of Clarksville's early town plan — a courthouse you find the way you find a secret. Its 1889 companion jail, by Haggart and Sanguinet, survives as the Old Jail Museum next door.
5. **Notable facts:** One of the oldest courthouses in Texas still in county use (1885); "only intact representative of architect W.H. Wilson" (THC); companion Victorian Italianate 1889 jail museum.
6. **Local pride hooks:** Clarksville's claim as a "gateway to Texas" for early Anglo settlers entering from the northeast (local claim); the restored clock tower.
7. **Sources:**

   - https://countyprogress.com/monuments-of-justice-red-river-county-courthouse/
   - https://architexas.com/projects/red-river-county-courthouse/

## Ranking

Ranked by story strength for shareable short-form documentary content, best first:

1. **Fayette County (La Grange)** — The Chicken Ranch is a nationally known story with a Broadway musical, a film, a crusading TV reporter, and a beloved sheriff; instantly shareable.
2. **Hood County (Granbury)** — A bartender on the square who confessed to being John Wilkes Booth, a touring mummy, and Kathy Bates's grandfather: irresistible mystery content.
3. **Hill County (Hillsboro)** — A New Year's Day fire, a fallen 1,525-pound bell, and Willie Nelson concerts that helped launch a statewide preservation movement; perfect redemption arc.
4. **Gonzales County** — A condemned man's curse on the clock that "never agrees," plus lightning strikes and the birthplace of the Texas Revolution; ideal spooky-season material.
5. **Ellis County (Waxahachie)** — The jilted stone carver legend told on 21 carved faces, in the "Best Little Hollywood in Texas"; visual storytelling writes itself.
6. **Donley County (Clarendon)** — "Saints' Roost" sobriety colony, the Panhandle's last legal hanging, 18 inches of bat guano, and a $3.1M resurrection; rich small-town texture.
7. **Shelby County (Center)** — The only Irish castle courthouse in America, built after a records fire in a county scarred by the Regulator–Moderator War; unique visual hook.
8. **Presidio County (Marfa)** — A pink Second Empire palace opened with a New Year's ball, later backdrop to Giant and the Marfa Lights; strong aesthetic and celebrity pull.
9. **Parker County (Weatherford)** — Oliver Loving's 600-mile journey home is the real Lonesome Dove; emotional cowboy storytelling anchored to the square's town.
10. **Caldwell County (Lockhart)** — Barbecue Capital of Texas plus Hollywood's favorite courthouse square; great lifestyle crossover, lighter on drama.
11. **Harrison County (Marshall)** — Clipped-then-restored eagles, a student fundraiser, and the Wonderland of Lights; charming, seasonal, but less of a single gripping tale.
12. **Red River County (Clarksville)** — A quietly moving "sole survivor" architecture story; beautiful but the least sensational narrative of the twelve.
