**Caption:**

When Howard Hughes died in 1976, no one could find a will.

Nevada litigated it. Document examiners on both sides testified. In 1978 a jury found the will was not written by Hughes.

That is the job. Not "trust me, I'm the expert." Show them.

**Headline (on image):**

> Sub: The paper gave it away.

**Torn-note callout:**

No swastikas, no insignia, no photographs of any person, no faces. Forensic lab mood, serious, clean.

**Caption:**

In 1983 a German magazine paid 9.3 million marks for sixty volumes of diaries it believed Adolf Hitler had written.

Historians vouched for them. Newspapers bought the rights. Within about two weeks the German federal archive had tested the materials and reported that the paper and ink were postwar. The diaries were fakes, produced in the early 1980s by a forger named Konrad Kujau, who went to prison for it.

The handwriting was close. The history had errors. But what ended the argument was the paper.

I tell clients this all the time. A skilled forger can practice a signature for months. What they can't do is change what the page is made of, or how ink ages, or the order in which lines were laid down.

Attorneys: if your questioned document is an original, ask your examiner what physical evidence is available beyond letterforms. Sometimes the strongest part of the opinion isn't the handwriting at all.

No faces, no people, no religious symbols or church imagery. Clinical, investigative, quiet.

**Caption:**

In the early 1980s a Salt Lake City document dealer named Mark Hofmann kept finding historic letters that experts had never seen.

They passed inspection. Buyers paid. In 1985 he killed two people with pipe bombs to keep the scheme from unraveling, and the investigation that followed put his documents under a microscope — literally.

What examiners found was that the ink on his "old" letters had a cracked surface, a pattern sometimes described as alligator skin. Genuine iron-gall ink that aged over a century doesn't do that. Ink that's been artificially aged does.

Hofmann pleaded guilty in 1987 and is still in prison.

I bring this one up because the documents had already fooled people who knew the field. Passing an initial look is not the same as passing an examination.

Attorneys: when the other side's expert says "it looks consistent," ask what they looked at, at what magnification, and what would have changed their mind. The answers tell you a lot.

## POST 5 — Joseph Cosey and the Lincoln Forgeries

**Headline (on image):**
> HE FORGED **LINCOLN** [gold: LINCOLN]
> Sub: For years. And some of it is still out there.

**Torn-note callout:**
> Active 1930s · Lincoln, Franklin, Poe · Old paper, mixed ink · Sold to collectors and libraries

**Image prompt:**
> Dark editorial graphic, 4:5 portrait. Deep navy ground with the texture of a library reading table. Right: a single aged letter sheet with confident 19th-century cursive and a flourished signature, partly under a magnifier; a dip pen, a small bottle of brown ink, and a stack of blank antique paper sheets beside it. Left: torn notepad callout. White bold headline "HE FORGED LINCOLN" with only "LINCOLN" in gold (#FFE455). No portraits, no faces, no currency, no monuments. Archival, warm-toned but serious.

**Caption:**

Joseph Cosey spent a good part of the 1930s writing letters from Abraham Lincoln. And Benjamin Franklin. And Edgar Allan Poe.

He used genuinely old paper. He mixed his own ink. He studied the handwriting closely enough that libraries and collectors bought his work, and some experts believe pieces of it are still sitting in collections today, catalogued as real.

What eventually gave him away wasn't one dramatic test. It was pattern. Examiners started comparing his "Lincolns" to each other and to authentic Lincoln writing, and the habits that were Cosey's — not Lincoln's — kept showing up.

That's the principle behind every comparison I do. No one writes exactly the same way twice, and no two people write exactly alike. A forger can copy the shape of a letter. The habits underneath are much harder to hide.

Attorneys: this is why the quantity and quality of known exemplars matters so much. Ask your examiner what they'd need to reach a firm opinion — and take the answer seriously.
