## How to build each image

The layout mirrors the reference post: dark ground, big headline with **exactly one gold word**, a torn-notebook callout on the left with 3–4 handwritten facts, the face on the right, a document or prop tying it to forensics, a short quote or sub-line beneath.

**On faces — the practical part.** Gemini and ChatGPT will usually refuse to generate a recognizable likeness of a real person, and when they don't refuse, the result is a synthetic "Aretha-ish" face, which reads as fake and looks worse than the real thing. So the workflow is:

1. Use the image prompt to generate the **design** — background, document, torn note, headline — with an empty photo zone on the right.
2. Drop a **real photograph** into that zone in Canva (or wherever the thumbnails get built).
3. Photo sourcing per post is listed below. I'm not a lawyer: pre-1929 photographs are public domain in the U.S., many government photos are too, and anything newer needs a license (Getty/AP editorial) or a public-domain source such as the Library of Congress or Wikimedia Commons with the license checked. Michigan, where Franklin's estate sits, recognizes post-mortem publicity rights — one more reason to license the Franklin photo rather than grab it.

**On logos.** The reference post uses Virgin and FUBU. The equivalents here are the *Stern* masthead (Post 3) and, if you want it, a newspaper front page. Using a magazine masthead in commentary about that magazine's own famous mistake is the kind of thing that's generally been fine, but it's a trademark on a marketing post — same "I'm not a lawyer" note applies.

Palette from the brand file: navy `#10142E`, white `#FFFFFF`, gold `#FFE455`, red `#B00708` for stamps only. **Hex values are flagged in the brand file as unverified best estimates** until someone checks `Generate_QDE_Forensic_Thumbnails_v3.sh`.

Suggested posting order: 1 → 3 → 2 → 4 → 5, one per week, with the "What I Look For" and "Before You Hire an Expert" series in the other slots.

**Caption (final, approved in chat):**

Aretha Franklin died in August 2018 with an estate of roughly six million dollars and, as far as anyone knew, no will.

Nine months later her niece, Sabrina Owens, went through the house. Aretha mostly lived in two rooms, the kitchen and the living room. Sabrina found a key to a cabinet nobody had opened since the funeral. Inside was an eleven-page handwritten will from 2010, notarized, stacked in with record contracts.

Then she got to the sofa. She lifted the far-right cushion. Three spiral notebooks.

One of them, in between the doodles, had a will dated March 2014. Parts of it were hard to read. Her son Ted's name was crossed out as executor and her youngest, Kecalf, was written in. The 2010 will had said the two younger sons had to finish business classes before they could inherit. The 2014 version dropped that. And at the bottom, her signature, with a smiley face drawn inside the "A."

The sons went to trial in Pontiac, Michigan, in July 2023. Ted's lawyer argued the obvious: the important one was under lock and key, and the other one was in a couch. Kecalf's lawyer asked him where his mother read her mail, signed papers, made her calls, and slept. He said, "on the couch."

Six jurors. Four poster-board blowups of the notebook pages. Under an hour of deliberation. The couch will won.

Here's the part worth sitting with. Nobody in that courtroom argued the notebook was forged. Ted testified it was his mother's handwriting. The fight was only about whether a page like that counts as a will under Michigan law.

Now picture the same case, and somebody says it isn't her handwriting.

That's a different question entirely. It doesn't get answered by where the paper was found or who had the key. It gets answered by comparing the questioned writing to known samples, under magnification, one habit at a time, and putting that comparison in front of the jury on an exhibit board.

I wasn't involved in the Franklin case. But I've examined a lot of handwritten wills where that exact argument was made, and in my experience it tends to surface late, after a family has already spent a year fighting about everything except the handwriting.

For the attorneys reading this: a holographic will usually raises three separate questions. Is the body of the writing hers. Is the signature hers. Is there anything on the page — a crossed-out name, a changed number — that was added by a different hand or at a different time. Those are three examinations, not one, and a declaration should treat them that way.

If you've got a handwritten will in dispute, get the handwriting question answered early. It changes how the rest of the case gets argued.

**Caption:**

Howard Hughes died in April 1976 on a plane over Texas, worth somewhere north of a billion dollars, and nobody could find a will.

Three weeks later, one turned up. Three handwritten pages, in an envelope, sitting on a desk at the headquarters of the LDS Church in Salt Lake City. Nobody saw who left it.

It split the estate into sixteen shares. One share to the church. One share to a man named "Melvin DuMar."

Melvin Dummar ran a gas station in Box Elder County, Utah. His story was that back in 1967 he'd found an old man lying in the Nevada desert, driven him to Las Vegas, and given him a quarter for a pay phone. The man said he was Howard Hughes.

At first Dummar said he had no idea how the will got to the church. Then the FBI found his thumbprint on the envelope. So the story changed: a stranger had dropped it at his gas station, he'd steamed it open, read it, and delivered it himself.

No one was ever charged. Hollywood made a movie about it two years later, and Dummar spent the rest of his life insisting he'd told the truth.

**Caption:**

In April 1983 the German magazine Stern announced it had Hitler's diaries. Sixty volumes. The story was that they'd gone down in a plane crash in the last days of the war, sat in a hayloft in East Germany for decades, and been quietly smuggled out.

Stern had paid 9.3 million marks for them. A famous British historian vouched for them. The Sunday Times bought the rights. Newsweek ran a cover.

The source was a Stuttgart antiques dealer calling himself Konrad Fischer. His real name was Konrad Kujau, and he'd written every page himself between 1981 and 1983, in a handwriting that looked a great deal like Hitler's.

Historians started finding problems in the content almost immediately. But what ended it was the German federal archive, the Bundesarchiv. They tested the paper. They tested the ink. They tested the glue and the bindings. Within about two weeks they announced the materials were postwar — the diaries had been written on paper that didn't exist when Hitler was alive, in ink that hadn't been made yet.

Kujau went to prison. So did the Stern reporter who'd brokered the deal. When Kujau confessed, he wrote out his confession in Hitler's handwriting, just to prove he could.

I wasn't involved in the Hitler diaries case — I was in high school. But I use it in my own training courses, because it makes a point I have to make to clients constantly.

A skilled forger can practice a signature for months and get close. What they can't do is change what the page is made of, how the ink oxidized, or which lines were laid down first. When the original document is available, the physical evidence sometimes says more than the handwriting does.

Attorneys: if your questioned document is an original, ask your examiner what they can look at beyond letterforms. Indentations, ink, paper, line sequence. Sometimes the strongest part of the opinion isn't the handwriting at all.

**Caption:**

In the early 1980s a young Salt Lake City document dealer named Mark Hofmann kept turning up things that weren't supposed to exist.

Letters from the founding of the Mormon church. A "salamander letter" that rewrote the story of how Joseph Smith found the golden plates. The only surviving copy of the first document ever printed in British America, which he was about to sell for over a million dollars. Collectors bought. The church bought. The FBI looked at some of it and passed it.

On the morning of October 15, 1985, a package bomb killed a collector named Steven Christensen at his office. A second bomb killed Kathleen Sheets at her home. The next day a third bomb went off in Hofmann's own car and nearly killed him.

The bombs were his. He'd built them to buy time, because a big deal was about to collapse and expose everything.

Now here's the part that belongs to my profession. Two document examiners, George Throckmorton and William Flynn, spent about sixteen months on nearly six hundred documents. And they found something nobody had caught.

Under the microscope, the ink on Hofmann's "old" documents had cracked. A fine, scaled pattern, like alligator skin. They pulled 461 genuine documents from the same period as a control group. None of them cracked. The cracking came from the chemicals he'd used to age his homemade iron-gall ink. They also noticed the ink had run slightly in one direction on every Hofmann piece — he'd hung them up to dry — and once they knew to look, they found the faint clip marks.

Hofmann pleaded guilty in 1987. He's still in prison.

I wasn't involved in the Hofmann case. I bring it up because the documents had already passed experts, archivists, and a federal lab. Passing an initial look is not the same as passing an examination.

Attorneys: when the other side's expert says "it looks consistent," ask what they compared it against, at what magnification, with what controls, and what would have changed their mind. The answers tell you a great deal.

## POST 5 — Joseph Cosey and the Lincoln Forgeries

**Headline (on image):**
> HE FORGED **LINCOLN** [gold: LINCOLN]
> Sub: For twenty years. Auction houses still sell his work — labeled.

Center-left: a single aged, folded letter sheet with confident 19th-century cursive and a flourished signature, partly under a round magnifier; a dip pen, a small bottle of brown ink, and a short stack of blank antique paper sheets beside it. **Caption:**

In 1929 a man named Martin Coneely walked into the Library of Congress, asked to see some old documents, and walked out with a pay warrant from 1786 signed by Benjamin Franklin.

He took it to a New York dealer to sell. The dealer looked at it and told him it was a fake.

It wasn't. But that moment changed his life, because he realized the people buying these things couldn't actually tell.

He took the name Joseph Cosey. He bought genuinely old paper. He mixed his own iron-gall ink to match the period. And for the better part of twenty years he produced letters, legal briefs, and manuscripts in the hands of Abraham Lincoln, Franklin, George Washington, Edgar Allan Poe — including a "draft" of the Declaration of Independence in Jefferson's handwriting and the "manuscript" of Poe's *The Black Cat*. He was arrested in 1937, served about a year, and went right back to it.

His Lincolns were good. The 1956 New Yorker profile said he'd papered the country with so many of them that the autograph market never fully recovered. Some are believed to still be in collections today, catalogued as real. Auction houses now sell known Cosey forgeries as their own category — a few hundred dollars each, labeled "Joseph Cosey: Abraham Lincoln."

I wasn't involved in the Cosey case; he died before I was born. But he's a textbook example of how a forger gets caught.

It wasn't one dramatic test. It was habits. His Lincoln letters ran too long — Lincoln was terse. His Franklin signatures looked like Franklin's from ten years too early. And when the document needed a second signature, a clerk's countersign, he barely bothered, and those didn't match the real clerks at all. Compare enough of his work side by side and the person underneath the imitation keeps showing up.

That's the principle behind every comparison I do. No one writes exactly the same way twice, and no two people write exactly alike. A forger can copy the shape of a letter. The habits underneath are much harder to hide, and they're what I'm looking for.

Attorneys: this is why I ask for as many known exemplars as you can get — checks, cards, contracts, anything in the person's ordinary hand. The opinion is only as strong as what I can compare against.
