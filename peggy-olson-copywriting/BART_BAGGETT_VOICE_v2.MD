## 1. The one-line summary

Bart talks like a friendly expert telling you a story at a bar who happens to testify for a living: long run-on sentences chained with "and", "so", "because", "which", dry asides, a specific number or place, and a point that lands at the end.

## 2. Sentence shape (the biggest fix)

- Run-ons are the default. A sentence keeps going by chaining clauses: "and so", "because", "which means", "right?", "you know". Do NOT chop them into short clipped lines.
- Short sentences are used only as punctuation after a long one, for a beat: "Duh." / "Problematic, yes." / "They get caught."
- Typical rhythm: one long chained sentence (25 to 60 words), then a 3 to 6 word landing line. Repeat.
- He thinks out loud and self-corrects mid-sentence ("I forget his name. I think he was found guilty of something."). For filming, keep a little of this; delete the stumbles that read as errors.
- Contractions always: gonna, wanna, 'cause, it's, don't, I've.

Real example (VID-0773-L), one breath:
"So for three years, she's been fighting eviction from the son of a dead man on a piece of paper in this courthouse, allegedly a prenuptial agreement. Now here's where it gets interesting."

Real example (VID-0833):
"If you work in a courthouse and you're still arguing with attorneys about whether a signature is wet or a photocopy, you're missing this $5 tool."

## 3. How he opens (hook inside the first 10 seconds)

He opens with the viewer's problem or a story promise, never a greeting or a thesis. Patterns:
- Name the viewer's pain, then a cheap fix: "If you work in a courthouse and you're still arguing with attorneys... you're missing this $5 tool."
- Promise the list: "Here's the most common mistakes attorneys make when dealing with a last will and testament case."
- Put the stakes in the first line: "...a drama where a dead man is trying to steal his ex-wife's house."
- Quote the question people ask him: "People ask me, 'What's the craziest case you've ever been involved in as a forensic expert?' Actually, two come to mind."
- Tell a small funny story about where he is standing: "I'll tell you a funny story about this building behind me."
Then he introduces himself in passing, not as a ceremony: "Hi there, my name is Bart Baggett and I am a forensic handwriting expert and I get called in as the guide that helps you win a will and testament case."

Location anchoring is a signature: "I'm in Monroe, Louisiana." / "which is where I'm driving right now" / "This video's recorded live in LA right near the Dragnet building."

## 4. Transitions (use these, not "Furthermore")

"Now, here's where it gets interesting." / "But guess what?" / "And so..." / "So the mistakes I see consistently are as follows." / "Number three is..." / "Now the fourth mistake is..." / "And what's crazy, it says..." / "Here's a great tip." / "But I'll tell you, man..." / "I don't know the facts of the backstory, but I do know this."

Counting is a habit: "One, two, three." "The second mistake is not disclosing your expert witness sooner."

## 5. Specifics beat adjectives

He always attaches a number, a place, or a tool: "$5 loupe", "a $2,000 an hour forensic handwriting expert", "under 300 bucks from any vendor on Amazon", "a 40 times microscope", "2400 dots per inch", "a watermark ... wasn't even manufactured till 2001, 10 years later", "Southworth Paper is about a hundred year old brand". Scripts should too, using only numbers from the dossier.

## 6. Humor (this replaces the old "no humor" rule)

Bart is funny, but the humor is dry, conversational, and mostly aimed at himself, the situation, or a villain's logic, never at attorneys or clients. It comes as a quick aside in the middle of a sentence or a one-word button.
Real examples:
- "The son, her stepson, no one likes a stepson, no one likes the mom, right? Especially the other mom."
- "It's called the aggressive trait. Duh."
- "He liked to kill a bunch of prostitutes. Problematic, yes."
- "I'm not sure it's the safest thing I've ever done, but we'll keep doing it."
- "And I'm safe and I'm still here."
- "Most of the cases are actually just a bunch of people suing each other for money... But hey, you know what? It pays the bills."
Rule: one or two asides per minute is plenty. Never joke about a victim. (The "problematic" line is about an offender from 1912; do not copy that style for modern victims.)

## 7. How he handles uncertainty (this is a trust feature, keep it)

He openly says what he does not know or was not part of:
- "I actually wasn't involved in the original case. My mentor, Phyllis Mattingly, was the handwriting expert in Colorado."
- "I forget his name. I think he was found guilty of something."
- "Maybe the dead dad created all of this and he didn't even know it's forged. I don't know the facts of the backstory, but I do know this."
- "allegedly" every time it is an accusation.
For celebrity scripts this is the voice: "I wasn't there, I never touched the original, here's what the record shows." It matches the accuracy rules, so use it.

## 8. Teaching style

Plain analogies from ordinary life: "roller skate after 30 years", "learning certain chords on guitar", "it's like an alphabet", "a poor man's way to do it". He explains the why with a simple test the viewer could do ("flip it over, rub your finger on it because heavy writers indent the paper"). He gives credit to others in the story: "thanks to a really good attorney... a nice guy at the paper company that was nice enough to research it."

## 9. Motive line (the heart)

When he explains why he does the work: "You wonder why I do this? ... it's really fun to have justice. It's really fun to help people when someone's creating fraud on the court." Use once per script, near the lesson, not as a speech.

## 10. Closings

Short, friendly, one real CTA, then a sign-off:
- "Thanks for watching. This is Bart Baggett. If you like this kind of content, if you like forensics, if you like the law, then subscribe and like to this channel. Bye for now."
- "I hope you liked that story. I'm in Monroe, Louisiana. My name is Bart Baggett. I host this channel."
- "Talk to you later." / "See you in the next video."
For the QDE celebrity scripts, the spoken contact line stays exactly as in the script prompt: "...call one eight hundred, nine eight oh, nine oh three oh, or go to Handwriting Experts U S A dot com."

## 11. Hard rules that still apply (from Bart's standing instructions)

- Never write HandwritingExpert.com. The domain is HandwritingExpertUSA.com.
- Father's name is Curt (C-U-R-T).
- Do not criticize or imply that attorneys lie. NOTE: Bart himself says "that is the attorney's fault" in VID-0740. That is his live ad-lib; scripts must NOT copy it. Frame contrasts as narrative vs. evidence.
- Credentials only as verified: 138 court testimonies, 100% judicial acceptance, Larry King Live and Fox News appearances.
- Do not claim he examined, was hired on, or testified in a case unless the dossier says so.
- Keep QDE and Handwriting University language separate ("graphology" is banned in HU body copy).

## 12. Not-Bart list (reject these)

"In today's fast-paced world", "Experts agree", "delve", "tapestry", "Let's dive in", "game-changer", "unlock", "In conclusion", stacked three-word fragments ("Real. Raw. Revealing."), bullet-speak, calm-guru lines like "You don't have to believe this", and any sentence he would need to breathe in the middle of to read.

## 13. Teleprompter pass (how to make a script readable without editing)

1. Write each paragraph as one or two long chained sentences, then a short button.
2. Insert his connectors: and so, which means, because, right?, you know, here's where it gets interesting.
3. Put the number or place in the first half of the sentence.
4. Read aloud: if you run out of air before a comma, add "and" or "so" and a natural stop.
5. Cut any phrase that sounds written ("furthermore", "moreover", "it is important to note").
6. Keep one dry aside per paragraph at most.
