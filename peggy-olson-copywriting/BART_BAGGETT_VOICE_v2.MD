## 1. The one-line summary

Bart talks like a friendly expert telling you a story at a bar who happens to testify for a living: long run-on sentences chained with "and", "so", "because", "which", dry asides, a specific number or place, and a point that lands at the end.

## 2. Sentence shape (the biggest fix)

- Run-ons are the default. A sentence keeps going by chaining clauses: "and so", "because", "which means", "right?", "you know". Do NOT chop them into short clipped lines.
- Short sentences are used only as punctuation after a long one, for a beat: "Duh." / "Problematic, yes." / "They get caught."
- Typical rhythm: one long chained sentence (25 to 60 words), then a 3 to 6 word landing line. Repeat. (The short-form Q-series answers are punchier; the long story videos are the run-on ones.)
- He thinks out loud and self-corrects mid-sentence. For filming, keep a little of this; delete the stumbles that read as errors.
- Contractions always: gonna, wanna, 'cause, it's, don't, I've.
- Loose numbers: "50 years", "like 50 years", "X plus". Do not over-round into corporate precision; use exact numbers only when the dossier gives them.

Real examples, one breath each:
"So for three years, she's been fighting eviction from the son of a dead man on a piece of paper in this courthouse, allegedly a prenuptial agreement. Now here's where it gets interesting."
"If you work in a courthouse and you're still arguing with attorneys about whether a signature is wet or a photocopy, you're missing this $5 tool."
"It's almost always the girlfriend, it's the caretaker or it's the brother that hasn't seen your friend in like 50 years and they show up with a will and say, 'Hey, he loved me. I get everything.'" (Q49)
"Now it's still in court because he was smart enough, or evil enough, to actually create a fake prenuptial agreement, file it in court, and then die and let his son work out the issues." (VID-0771a)

## 3. How he opens (hook inside the first 10 seconds)

He opens with the viewer's problem, a question he gets asked, or a story promise. Never a greeting or a thesis. Patterns:
- Question then "Well...": "Can a forensic handwriting expert testify in court? Well, of course, that's why they pay us." (Q06)
- "Common question, what if my signature changes all the time?" (Q05)
- "How long does it take to analyze handwriting for a forgery case? I get this question all the time because everyone has a legal case." (Q08)
- Short teaser: "Anonymous letters. Some of my favorite cases." (Q46)
- Stakes plus place: "How do you screw over your ex-wife from the dead? Well, I'm here in Monroe, Louisiana, watching that happen." (VID-0771)
- Viewer pain, cheap fix: "If you work in a courthouse and you're still arguing with attorneys... you're missing this $5 tool."
- Quote the question people ask him: "People ask me, 'What's the craziest case you've ever been involved in as a forensic expert?' Actually, two come to mind."
Then he introduces himself in passing, not as a ceremony: "Hi there, my name is Bart Baggett and I am a forensic handwriting expert..."

Location anchoring is a signature: "I'm in Monroe, Louisiana." / "This video's recorded live in LA right near the Dragnet building."

## 4. Transitions (use these, not "Furthermore")

"So the answer is..." (his most frequent bridge after a question) / "Now, here's where it gets interesting." / "But guess what?" / "And so..." / "But here's what doesn't change." / "And here's why." / "That's a whole different case." / "Number three is..." / "Here's a great tip." / "I don't know the facts of the backstory, but I do know this."

Counting is a habit: "One, two, three." "The second mistake is not disclosing your expert witness sooner."

## 5. Specifics beat adjectives

He always attaches a number, a place, or a tool: "$5 loupe", "a $2,000 an hour forensic handwriting expert", "2400 dots per inch", "a watermark ... wasn't even manufactured till 2001, 10 years later". Scripts should too, using only numbers from the dossier.

## 6. Humor (this replaces the old "no humor" rule)

Dry, conversational, mostly aimed at himself, the situation, or a villain's logic. Never at attorneys, clients, or victims. A quick aside mid-sentence or a one-word button.
Real examples:
- "The son, her stepson, no one likes a stepson, no one likes the mom, right? Especially the other mom."
- "That's a little inside baseball right there."
- "I don't know anyone that uses faxes, but some people do."
- "It's called the aggressive trait. Duh."
- "Most of the cases are actually just a bunch of people suing each other for money... But hey, you know what? It pays the bills."
Rule: one or two asides per minute is plenty. ("Problematic, yes" is about a historical offender; do not copy that style for modern victims.)

## 7. How he handles uncertainty (a trust feature, keep it)

- "I actually wasn't involved in the original case. My mentor, Phyllis Mattingly, was the handwriting expert in Colorado."
- "I don't remember how that case turned out."
- "I don't know the facts of the backstory, but I do know this."
- "...we're not hired to help you win your case. We're hired to tell the truth."
- "allegedly" every time it is an accusation.
For celebrity scripts: "I wasn't there, I never touched the original, here's what the record shows."

## 8. Teaching style

Plain analogies: "learning guitar learning the basic notes", "roller skate after 30 years", "it's like an alphabet". He explains the why with a test the viewer could do ("flip it over, rub your finger on it because heavy writers indent the paper"). He gives credit to others in the story.

## 9. Motive line (the heart)

"You wonder why I do this? ... it's really fun to have justice." / "I speak for dead people... I feel the same way about documents." / "So it's a pretty cool business. We get paid to tell the truth." Use once per script, near the lesson, not as a speech.

## 10. Closings

His real sign-offs follow one formula: name, a light ask, a goodbye.
- "My name is Bart Baggett. If this has been helpful in any way, hit like and subscribe... Talk to you later. Thanks for watching."
- "All right, my name's Bart Baggett. Like, comment below, reach out if you need some help. Ciao."
- "Thanks for watching. This is Bart Baggett... Bye for now."
For QDE celebrity scripts, the spoken contact line stays exactly as in the script prompt: "...call one eight hundred, nine eight oh, nine oh three oh, or go to Handwriting Experts U S A dot com."

## 11. Hard rules that still apply

- Never write HandwritingExpert.com. The domain is HandwritingExpertUSA.com.
- Father's name is Curt (C-U-R-T).
- Do not criticize or imply that attorneys lie. Bart ad-libs "that is the attorney's fault" (VID-0740) and "There's two people. One of them is lying" (Q54); scripts must NOT copy those. Frame contrasts as narrative vs. evidence.
- Credentials only as verified: 138 court testimonies, 100% judicial acceptance, Larry King Live and Fox News appearances.
- Do not claim he examined, was hired on, or testified in a case unless the dossier says so.
- Dates: he sometimes misstates dates on camera. Never copy a date from a transcript; use the dossier.
- QDE and Handwriting University stay separate ("graphology" is banned in HU body copy). HU videos (VID-0106, 0110, 0758, 0790, 0819, 0821, 0827) were used for rhythm only.

## 12. Not-Bart list (reject these)

"In today's fast-paced world", "Experts agree", "delve", "tapestry", "Let's dive in", "game-changer", "unlock", "In conclusion", stacked three-word fragments ("Real. Raw. Revealing."), bullet-speak, calm-guru lines like "You don't have to believe this", and any sentence he would need to breathe in the middle of to read.

## 13. Teleprompter pass

1. Write each paragraph as one or two long chained sentences, then a short button.
2. Insert his connectors: and so, which means, because, right?, you know, so the answer is, here's where it gets interesting.
3. Put the number or place in the first half of the sentence.
4. Read aloud: if you run out of air before a comma, add "and" or "so" and a natural stop.
5. Cut any phrase that sounds written ("furthermore", "moreover", "it is important to note").
6. One dry aside per paragraph at most.
