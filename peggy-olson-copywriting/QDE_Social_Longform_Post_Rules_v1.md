**Owner:** Peggy (copy). **Created:** 2026-09-28.

**Why this file exists:** on 2026-09-28 Bart rewrote the Facebook and LinkedIn posts for Sept 29 (Newburyport courthouse) and Sept 30 (Dreyfus) by hand. The pipeline's versions were too short, too obviously a sales pitch, and had no story or hook for a human reader. The cause was the old rules: Facebook Post 100–200 words, LinkedIn 150–300 words, a phone-number sales close forced onto every post, and famous-case rows reusing the short Instagram caption. **This file is the only rule set for these two posts.** Bart's two rewrites (below) are the standard.

**Does NOT apply to:** Facebook Reels, TikTok, Instagram, YouTube. Those stay short. Leave them alone.

---

## 1. LENGTH

- **350–600 words** of body, before the sign-off and hashtags.
- Length comes from story detail, not filler. Do not repeat yourself to reach the floor.
- If the source cannot honestly support 350 words, write what it supports and add **`THIN SOURCE — under 350 words`** to `to_verify`. A human decides. Never invent to fill.

## 2. THE FIRST TWO LINES (the hook)

Facebook and LinkedIn cut the post off after about two lines behind "See more". Those two lines must make a stranger want the rest.

- Open on a **scene, a stake, or a turn**, not a topic and not a sales line.
  - Good: "In 1894, a torn piece of paper helped send an innocent man to prison for treason."
  - Good: "A few months ago, I had one of those experiences that reminds me how unusual my work can be."
- Banned openers: "Attorneys:", "Are you dealing with…", "If you need a handwriting expert…", "Call…", "Did you know…", "In this video…".
- A "Forensic Files" tension line is **allowed** here, on LinkedIn too.

## 3. THE BODY: TELL IT AS A STORY

- In order: who, where, when, what was at stake, what happened, then **the turn** ("Then Alphonse Bertillon entered the case." / "But the case began to unravel.").
- Concrete specifics: names, places, dates, numbers, the object (the torn note, the spiral notebook, the 1805 courthouse). Specifics are what make it credible.
- Short paragraphs: 1 to 3 sentences each, a blank line between them.
- Plain words. No hype words (shocking, unbelievable, secret, exposed, jaw-dropping). No fake urgency. Few em dashes.

## 4. THE LESSON (the last few paragraphs)

- Turn the story into one clear point about evidence, credentials or method. Examples from Bart: "Credentials matter. So does methodology." / "I tell the truth about a document. The jury decides who is lying."
- End on a short line that lands the point ("What he could never get back were the years the case had taken from him.").

## 5. FIRST PERSON: WHAT BART MAY SAY

Write as Bart, in first person. But **never invent Bart's opinions, memories, feelings or experiences.**

Allowed:
- Anything Bart says in the video transcript (video posts).
- "I wasn't involved in the ___ case." / "I obviously wasn't around to examine the ___."
- Plain statements of what the job is: "My job is to examine the handwriting and the documents and explain what the evidence shows."
- Verified credentials only: 138 court testimonies, 100% judicial acceptance, federal/civil/criminal/probate/international courts, Larry King Live and Fox News.

Banned (examples of real mistakes):
- "It is the one I think about when an examiner sounds certain."
- "If I were the examiner…", "I've always believed…", "I took one look and knew…" (unless Bart said it in the transcript or wrote it himself).

## 6. FACTS

- **Video posts:** every claim must trace to the transcript. Well-known public background about a named place or case (for example, the courthouse's date or architect) may be added **only if each added fact is listed in `to_verify`**.
- **Famous-case posts:** use only the sourced narrative supplied from the sheet. Never use lines marked with a warning. Never state or imply a living person committed a crime unless the narrative says they were convicted.
- Names and spellings per `Peggy_Copy_Names_and_Spelling_v1.md`. Never "Kurt" (it is Curt). Never "graphology".

## 7. THE ENDING: NO SALES PITCH

- No phone number, no "Call our office", no "Case consultations", no "Attorneys: ask…" line. Bart's standard is that the story sells; the close does not.
- After the last line, the sign-off block, exactly:

```
Bart Baggett
bartbaggett.com
```

- Then **4–6 hashtags on one line**, professional, for example `#ForensicHandwriting #QuestionedDocuments #ExpertWitness #HandwritingExpert` plus one case tag (`#DreyfusAffair`). Never `#TrueCrime` or `#LegalTok` on LinkedIn.
- Never write `HandwritingExpert.com` (singular). The owned domains are HandwritingExpertUSA.com and bartbaggett.com.
- No cross-platform pointers ("link in bio", "watch on YouTube").

## 8. FACEBOOK POST AND LINKEDIN MAY BE THE SAME TEXT

Bart's own rewrites use the same story on both. That is fine. If you vary them, the LinkedIn version may add one line aimed at attorneys **inside the story**, never as a sales close.

## 9. CHECKLIST BEFORE RETURNING

1. First two lines would stop a stranger? Scene, stake or turn, not a pitch?
2. 350–600 words, or `THIN SOURCE` flagged?
3. A real story with names, places, dates and a turn?
4. Lesson in the last paragraphs, ending on a short line?
5. No invented Bart opinions, memories or feelings?
6. No phone number, no "call", no "Attorneys:" close? Sign-off block exact?
7. 4–6 hashtags, domains spelled right?

### Example A: video post (Newburyport courthouse, Sept 29, 2026)

A few months ago, I had one of those experiences that reminds me how unusual my work can be.

I found myself in Newburyport, Massachusetts, to testify in a handwriting forgery case.
The courthouse itself turned out to be almost as interesting as the case.

The Superior Courthouse in Newburyport dates to 1805 and has been described by the Massachusetts court system as the oldest courthouse in the United States still in continuous service.

It was designed by Charles Bulfinch, the architect who also designed the Massachusetts State House.

Daniel Webster argued cases there. So did Caleb Cushing, who went on to become United States Attorney General. Historical records also connect John Quincy Adams with arguing cases in this courthouse.

That is quite a list of lawyers to follow.

And then there was me...
Walking into the same courthouse more than two centuries later to talk about handwriting and whether a document was authentic. Same wooden benches. Same small rooms where attorneys negotiated settlements.

There was something about that I enjoyed.

Generations of lawyers, judges, witnesses, and jurors have walked through those doors carrying some version of the same basic problem.

Two people disagree about what happened. Both may be absolutely certain. Eventually, somebody has to look at the evidence.

In my case, the issue involved an alleged forgery.
That is where my work becomes very practical.

I am not there to decide who is a good person or a bad person. I am not there to decide who deserves to win. My job is much narrower. I tell the truth about a document. The jury decides who is lying.

Examine the handwriting. Examine the documents. Look at the evidence. Then explain what I found clearly enough that a judge or jury can understand it.

Sometimes the stakes are surprisingly high.

Last month, I worked on a handwriting matter involving a man facing a four-year jail sentence.

I have spent most of my life in the South and California, so I haven't spent nearly as much time on the East Coast. Newburyport was a pleasant surprise.

Beautiful town. Beautiful old courthouse.
And a reminder that while the places change and the technology changes, the underlying questions in a courtroom really haven't changed very much.

What happened?
What can be proven?
And what does the evidence actually show?

Those were important questions in 1805.
They are still important in 2026.

For me, that is still the interesting part of this work: going wherever the documents take me and helping a court get a little closer to the truth.

Bart Baggett
bartbaggett.com

### Example B: famous-case post (Dreyfus, Sept 30, 2026)

(Bart posted this one with hashtags only. From now on, every post adds the sign-off block from section 7 above the hashtags.)

In 1894, a torn piece of paper helped send an innocent man to prison for treason.
Captain Alfred Dreyfus was a French artillery officer when French intelligence recovered pieces of an unsigned note from the German embassy in Paris. Known as the Bordereau, it offered French military secrets to the Germans.

With no signature identifying the author, investigators turned to the handwriting.
But the experts did not agree.

Alfred Gobert, handwriting expert for the Bank of France, found numerous and important differences between the Bordereau and Dreyfus's known handwriting. He concluded Dreyfus did not write it. Another examiner, Pelletier, also excluded him.

Then Alphonse Bertillon entered the case.

Bertillon was a respected specialist in criminal identification, but he was not a handwriting expert. Yet he developed an elaborate theory that Dreyfus had disguised his own handwriting.
Here is where the reasoning became dangerous.

Instead of treating the differences as evidence that someone else might have written the document, Bertillon used those differences to support his theory that Dreyfus had intentionally disguised his writing.

In other words, evidence contradicting the theory became evidence supporting it.
Dreyfus was convicted of treason in December 1894, publicly stripped of his military rank, and imprisoned on Devil's Island.

But the case began to unravel.

Evidence pointed toward another French officer, Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy, as the author of the Bordereau. Dreyfus was eventually returned to France for another trial in 1899.
Remarkably, he was convicted again.

He received a presidential pardon ten days later, but a pardon was not an exoneration. His name still had not been cleared.

The handwriting evidence also came under greater scrutiny. In 1904, mathematicians Henri Poincaré, Paul Appell, and Gaston Darboux examined Bertillon's methods and found serious problems with his reasoning.

Finally, on July 12, 1906, France's highest court quashed Dreyfus's conviction. Nearly twelve years after his arrest, he was exonerated and later reinstated in the French Army.

I obviously wasn't around to examine the Bordereau, but after years of examining disputed handwriting and testifying about questioned documents, this case still makes an important point.
Credentials matter. So does methodology.

Bertillon was accomplished in his own field. But expertise in one forensic discipline does not automatically make someone an expert in another.

For Dreyfus, that distinction had devastating consequences.

He eventually got his freedom, his military rank, and his name back.
What he could never get back were the years the case had taken from him.

When someone's freedom, reputation, property, or future may depend on a few lines of handwriting, getting the examination right matters.
